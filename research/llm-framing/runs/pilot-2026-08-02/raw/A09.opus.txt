Humans became dominant through a combination of traits that individually appear in other animals but together proved transformative. Bipedalism freed the hands, and dexterous hands made tool use possible. Large, energy-hungry brains supported planning, memory, and abstract reasoning, sustained by cooking, which unlocked more calories from food. Most decisive was cumulative culture: humans learned from one another with enough fidelity that knowledge accumulated across generations rather than being reinvented. No individual could design a rifle or a vaccine from scratch, but populations connected by language and teaching could refine techniques over centuries. Language also enabled cooperation among large groups of unrelated strangers, coordinated by shared stories, norms, and institutions, whereas most species cooperate only with kin or small familiar bands. Add extreme behavioral flexibility, allowing adaptation to deserts, ice sheets, and rainforests without waiting for genetic change, and humans could occupy nearly every terrestrial environment and reshape it deliberately to their own purposes.
