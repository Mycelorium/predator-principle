Speciation begins when populations stop exchanging genes freely. Geographic barriers — a river shifting course, a mountain rising, a few birds blown to an island — split a population, and drift plus divergent selection push the isolated groups apart until their genomes no longer mesh. Divergence can also happen without barriers: populations exploiting different food sources, breeding seasons, or mating signals can split while still in contact. Competition drives much of this. Where similar species overlap, selection favors individuals that differ most from rivals, exaggerating differences in beak size, body form, or courtship display. Predators and parasites add pressure, favoring novel defenses. But cooperation matters too. Symbiosis — corals with algae, insects with gut bacteria, plants with fungi — opens ecological niches that neither partner could occupy alone, and shifts in partners can isolate host lineages. Hybridization and whole-genome duplication, common in plants, can produce a new species in a single generation. Sexual selection, social learning of songs, and cultural traditions further accelerate the process.
