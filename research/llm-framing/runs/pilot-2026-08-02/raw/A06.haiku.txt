Ants and termites dominate terrestrial ecosystems through eusocial organization, where individuals sacrifice reproduction for colony benefit. This generates extraordinary efficiency: specialized castes optimize foraging, defense, and reproduction; chemical communication coordinates complex behaviors across thousands to millions of members. Their success stems from several factors. First, colonies achieve remarkable resilience—losing individual workers barely impacts colony function. Second, they exploit diverse food sources: ants are predatory and omnivorous, while termites digest cellulose through microbial symbiosis, accessing resources unavailable to most competitors. Third, their distributed intelligence enables rapid adaptation to environmental changes. Fourth, colonies occupy numerous ecological niches simultaneously, from soil to canopies. Finally, their reproductive strategy concentrates resources into colony growth rather than individual offspring, maximizing population expansion. The synergy of cooperative behavior, metabolic efficiency, dietary flexibility, and adaptive capacity makes ants and termites among Earth's most successful animal lineages, comprising roughly 20% of terrestrial animal biomass.
