Coral reefs pack extraordinary biodiversity into small areas mainly because the corals themselves build complex three-dimensional structures. Branching, plating, and boulder-shaped colonies create countless crevices, overhangs, and surfaces, so a single patch of reef offers many distinct microhabitats — shelter for tiny fish and shrimp, hunting perches for predators, and hard substrate for sponges, algae, and invertebrates. This architectural complexity lets many species partition space and food finely rather than compete head-on. Reefs also thrive in warm, clear, nutrient-poor tropical waters where the symbiosis between corals and photosynthetic zooxanthellae captures sunlight and injects energy into an otherwise sparse system, while efficient internal recycling keeps nutrients circulating rather than being lost. Add long evolutionary stability in tropical seas, high larval dispersal connecting distant reefs, and intense predation and grazing that prevent any one competitor from dominating, and the result is among the richest ecosystems on Earth.
