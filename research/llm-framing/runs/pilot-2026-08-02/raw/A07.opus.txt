Evolution is driven by a handful of interacting forces. Natural selection is the central one: heritable variants that improve survival and reproduction in a given environment become more common over generations, producing adaptation. Mutation supplies the raw variation — random changes in DNA from copying errors, radiation, or chemical damage — without which selection would have nothing to act on. Genetic recombination during sexual reproduction reshuffles existing variants into new combinations. Genetic drift causes allele frequencies to fluctuate randomly, an effect that dominates in small populations and can fix or eliminate traits regardless of their usefulness. Gene flow, the migration of individuals and their genes between populations, spreads variation and counteracts divergence. Beyond these, non-random mating and sexual selection shape which traits are passed on, while geographic isolation and ecological pressures drive populations apart into new species. Together these processes explain both gradual adaptation and the branching diversity of life.
