Gut bacteria and their host are locked in a constant, two-way exchange. The animal supplies a warm, nutrient-rich habitat and a steady flow of undigested food; the microbes, in return, break down complex polysaccharides and fibres the host cannot digest, releasing short-chain fatty acids like butyrate and acetate that feed gut cells and supply energy. They synthesise vitamins such as K and several B vitamins, metabolise bile acids, and detoxify certain plant compounds. They also occupy space and consume resources that would otherwise be available to pathogens, and produce antimicrobial substances, providing colonisation resistance. Crucially, they instruct the immune system: microbial molecules shape the development of gut lymphoid tissue and regulatory T cells, tuning tolerance and inflammation. Signals travel further afield too, influencing metabolism, fat storage, and even behaviour via the gut-brain axis. The relationship is conditional rather than purely benign: when community composition or barrier integrity breaks down, the same organisms can drive inflammation and disease.
