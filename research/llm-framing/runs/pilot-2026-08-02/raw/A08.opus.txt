An ecosystem's structure emerges from the interplay of physical conditions and biological interactions. Climate — temperature, rainfall, seasonality — sets the broad envelope, while soil chemistry, topography, water availability, and light determine what can grow where. Within that envelope, energy flow shapes the architecture: primary producers capture sunlight, and the efficiency with which energy passes upward limits how many trophic levels can be supported and how much biomass each holds. Nutrient cycling, driven largely by decomposers, replenishes what growth consumes.

Species interactions then sculpt the details. Competition partitions resources into niches, predation and herbivory regulate abundances, and mutualisms like pollination and mycorrhizal partnerships underpin whole communities. Keystone species and ecosystem engineers exert influence far beyond their numbers. Disturbance — fire, flood, storms, grazing — resets succession and maintains patchiness, often sustaining diversity that would otherwise collapse under dominance. Layered onto all this are historical contingency, dispersal limits, and, increasingly, human land use and climate change.
