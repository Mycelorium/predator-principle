Trees in nutrient-poor forests employ several strategies to acquire phosphorus and nitrogen. The most critical is their symbiotic relationship with mycorrhizal fungi, which colonize tree roots and extend far into the soil, dramatically increasing the root system's absorptive surface area. These fungi exchange absorbed nutrients for photosynthetic sugars from the tree. Some trees also harbor nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules or form associations with nitrogen-fixing microorganisms in the soil, converting atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms. Nutrient cycling through decomposition of leaf litter and dead organic matter is another vital source—trees produce phosphatase enzymes to break down complex organic phosphorus compounds. Additionally, trees can access nutrients released through slow weathering of parent rock material in the soil. In some nutrient-poor ecosystems, trees also benefit from nutrient redistribution through fungal networks that connect multiple trees, allowing resource sharing. These integrated strategies enable tree survival despite extremely limited nutrient availability in the surrounding environment.
