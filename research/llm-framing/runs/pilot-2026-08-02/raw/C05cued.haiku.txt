New species arise through reproductive isolation, which occurs via both competitive and cooperative processes. Competitive mechanisms include natural selection, where divergent environments favor different traits, and sexual selection, where populations develop incompatible mate preferences. Resource competition can drive populations into different niches, accumulating genetic differences that eventually prevent interbreeding. Geographic isolation amplifies these competitive pressures. Conversely, cooperative mechanisms also drive speciation: symbiotic relationships between organisms can lead to coevolution and reproductive isolation. Horizontal gene transfer, particularly in microbes, introduces genetic variation that creates reproductive barriers. Mutualistic relationships can diverge into specialized partnerships incompatible with other variants. Additionally, endosymbiosis demonstrates how cooperation can generate entirely new cellular lineages. Both mechanisms ultimately reduce gene flow between populations. Competitive divergence emphasizes individual fitness and environmental adaptation, while cooperative divergence highlights how relationships and mutual dependencies can create reproductive isolation. Most speciation events likely involve both processes, with competitive pressures in external environments complemented by cooperative relationships that reinforce reproductive incompatibility.
