Plants moved onto land in the Ordovician, roughly 470 million years ago, from freshwater green algae ancestors, and several innovations made it possible. A waxy cuticle sealed the surface against desiccation, while stomata — pores with guard cells — let carbon dioxide in while limiting water loss. Rhizoids and later true roots anchored plants and drew up water and minerals, aided by symbiosis with fungi, which effectively extended the absorbing surface into the substrate. Lignin and other strengthening compounds gave stems the rigidity to stand upright without water's buoyancy, and vascular tissue (xylem and phloem) transported water upward and sugars throughout, allowing greater size. Spores with tough sporopollenin walls, and eventually pollen and seeds, freed reproduction from standing water. Environmental conditions helped too: an ozone layer screening ultraviolet radiation, high atmospheric carbon dioxide, and unexploited, competitor-free ground with weathering rock supplying nutrients.
