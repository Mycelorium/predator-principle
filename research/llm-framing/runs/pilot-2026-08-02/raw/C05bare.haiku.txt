New species arise through a process called speciation, which occurs over long periods when populations of organisms become isolated and accumulate genetic differences. The primary mechanism is allopatric speciation, where geographic barriers such as mountains, rivers, or distance separate populations, preventing gene flow between them. As these isolated populations experience different environmental pressures and random genetic mutations, they evolve distinct traits and adaptations. Eventually, the genetic differences become so substantial that even if populations reunite, they can no longer interbreed successfully, defining them as separate species. Other speciation modes include sympatric speciation, where new species evolve within the same geographic area through mechanisms like polyploidy in plants or behavioral isolation. The process relies on natural selection favoring traits suited to local environments, coupled with genetic drift and mutation. Reproductive isolation—the inability to produce viable, fertile offspring with other groups—is the key criterion distinguishing a new species, marking the completion of speciation.
