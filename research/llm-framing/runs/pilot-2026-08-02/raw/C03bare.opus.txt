Soil microbial communities run on a continuous supply of carbon and energy from plants and dead organic matter. Roots leak sugars, organic acids, and amino acids into the rhizosphere, feeding bacteria and fungi that in turn mineralize nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur into forms the plant can absorb. Beyond that, decomposers break down litter, dead roots, and each other's necromass, so the same carbon atoms cycle repeatedly through successive generations. Metabolic division of labor is central: one organism's waste product, whether acetate, methane, ammonium, or reduced sulfur, is another's substrate, chaining together across steep oxygen and redox gradients within soil aggregates. Many cells persist as dormant spores or in low-activity states for years, reviving when moisture and fresh substrate arrive. Physical structure matters too, since pore spaces and mineral surfaces hold water, protect organic matter, and create countless microhabitats. Predation by protists and nematodes recycles nutrients and keeps the whole system turning over.
