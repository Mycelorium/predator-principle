Coral reefs pack enormous diversity into nutrient-poor tropical water largely because the corals themselves build three-dimensional habitat. The calcium carbonate framework creates countless crevices, overhangs, and microhabitats that differ in light, flow, and shelter, so species can partition space finely instead of competing head-on. That partitioning extends to diet and timing, with nocturnal and diurnal fishes using the same structures in shifts. Competition is intense — corals sting, overgrow, and shade rivals — but constant disturbance from storms, predation, and grazing fish keeps any single dominant from monopolizing space, leaving openings for weaker competitors and sustaining a shifting mosaic. Cooperation is equally central: reef-building corals host symbiotic algae that supply most of their energy, allowing productivity where nutrients are scarce, and the reef is dense with mutualisms such as cleaner wrasse servicing client fish, anemonefish, and gobies guarding shrimp burrows. Tight nutrient recycling among these partners sustains the whole system.
