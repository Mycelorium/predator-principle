Ecosystem structure is shaped by interconnected physical, biological, and chemical factors. Abiotic conditions—climate, temperature, precipitation, light, and soil composition—determine which organisms can survive in a given environment and establish its baseline productivity. Biotic factors, including the diversity and abundance of species, create complex food webs and trophic levels that organize energy flow from primary producers through consumers to decomposers. Species interactions such as competition, predation, and symbiosis regulate population sizes and community composition. Nutrient cycling, involving the movement of elements like nitrogen and phosphorus through organisms and the physical environment, sustains ecosystem functioning. Ecological succession—the predictable changes in community composition over time—further refines structure, while geographic location and evolutionary history influence which species occupy available ecological niches. Together, these factors create unique, dynamic systems where energy flows and nutrients cycle through layers of interdependent organisms adapted to their specific conditions.
