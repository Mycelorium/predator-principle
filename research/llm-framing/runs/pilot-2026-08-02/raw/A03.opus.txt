Multicellularity arose independently more than twenty times, so the transition was clearly not a fluke. It typically begins when single cells fail to separate after dividing, forming clumps or filaments of genetically identical clones — something seen today in choanoflagellates, the closest living relatives of animals, which form rosettes in response to bacterial cues. Because the cells are clones, cooperation is favored by kin selection: a cell that sacrifices its own reproduction still propagates its genes through its neighbors. Group living then pays immediate dividends, such as escaping predators too small to swallow a cluster, improving feeding currents, or sharing nutrients. Once groups persist, selection begins acting on the collective rather than the individual, favoring division of labor. Cells specialize, and crucially a germ line is set aside while somatic cells give up reproduction entirely. Existing genetic toolkits for adhesion, cell signaling, and differentiation, already present in single-celled ancestors, were repurposed to coordinate the new bodies.
