In nutrient-poor forests, trees rely heavily on partnerships and tight recycling rather than on free mineral nutrients in the soil. Most fine roots are colonized by mycorrhizal fungi — ectomycorrhizae on many temperate and boreal trees, arbuscular mycorrhizae elsewhere — whose hyphae extend far beyond the root's depletion zone, exploring litter and soil pores too fine for roots to enter. The fungi release phosphatases and organic acids that liberate phosphate bound to iron, aluminium and calcium minerals or locked in organic matter, and some ectomycorrhizal and ericoid fungi secrete proteases that extract nitrogen directly from proteins, amino acids and chitin in decaying litter, short-circuiting the usual mineralization step. In exchange the tree supplies sugars, often a large share of its photosynthate. Additional nitrogen arrives from symbiotic and free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria, canopy epiphytes, and atmospheric deposition. Trees also conserve what they have: nutrients are resorbed from leaves before shedding, and roots proliferate into decomposing litter and coarse woody debris.
