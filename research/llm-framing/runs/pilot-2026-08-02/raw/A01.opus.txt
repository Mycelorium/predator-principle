The leading explanation is endosymbiosis. Roughly two billion years ago, a host cell — most likely an archaeon, related to the Asgard archaea recently found to have complex cytoskeletons and membrane-remodelling proteins — engulfed or otherwise permanently captured a free-living alphaproteobacterium, a relative of today's Rickettsia. Instead of being digested, the bacterium survived inside and was retained, probably because the partnership was metabolically profitable: the guest could perform oxygen-based respiration, extracting far more energy from food than fermentation, while the host supplied nutrients and a stable environment. Over many generations the endosymbiont lost its independence, transferring most of its genes to the host nucleus and keeping only a small circular genome, its own double membrane and its own ribosomes — features that still betray its bacterial ancestry. The resulting cheap, scalable energy supply is widely argued to have permitted the large genomes and elaborate internal structure that define eukaryotes. Chloroplasts arose later by a comparable capture of cyanobacteria.
