Long-run productivity rests on a stock of useful knowledge and on institutions that let that knowledge be applied. The competitive side is the familiar one: firms rivalrously innovate to win customers, entry and exit reallocate labour and capital from weak producers to strong ones, and open markets expose incumbents to pressure they cannot lobby away. Much measured productivity growth comes from this churn rather than from existing firms improving in place. But competition alone under-supplies the inputs it feeds on. Basic research, education, standards, legal enforcement, public infrastructure and trust between trading partners are cooperative goods — costly to produce, hard to appropriate privately, and prone to collapse when everyone free-rides. Dense networks of collaboration, from supplier relationships to scientific communities to industry-wide training schemes, spread tacit know-how that no single firm would fund. Sustained productivity therefore looks like competition operating inside a scaffold of cooperation, with each mechanism correcting the other's characteristic failure.
