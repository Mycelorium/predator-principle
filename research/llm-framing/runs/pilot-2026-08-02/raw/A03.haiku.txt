Multicellular organisms evolved from single-celled organisms through a process spanning hundreds of millions of years. Early unicellular life forms, particularly early eukaryotes, began forming loose aggregates or colonies around 1.2 billion years ago. These colonial arrangements offered survival advantages: specialized cells could perform different functions, predators struggled to consume organized groups, and larger size provided protection from ultraviolet radiation.

Over time, cell divisions became increasingly coordinated. Cells in colonies began differentiating into specialized types—some for reproduction, others for nutrient absorption or protection. Adhesion molecules kept cells bonded together, while chemical signaling enabled intercellular communication. This division of labor improved efficiency and survival prospects.

Fossil evidence shows early sponges and simple multicellular organisms emerged roughly 600-700 million years ago. Natural selection favored organisms with better cellular coordination and specialization. Eventually, complex developmental programs evolved to guide multicellular growth from single fertilized cells. This transition fundamentally transformed life's complexity, enabling the evolution of plants, animals, fungi, and the biodiversity we observe today.
