New species arise when populations of a single species stop exchanging genes and diverge until they can no longer interbreed. Most commonly this begins with geographic separation: a river shifts, a glacier advances, a few birds colonize an island. Once isolated, the populations accumulate different mutations, and natural selection pushes them toward different local optima while genetic drift shuffles the rest at random. Over many generations the genetic and developmental differences compound, and eventually the two groups become reproductively incompatible — their offspring may be inviable, sterile, or simply never produced because courtship signals, flowering times, or habitats no longer match. Speciation can also happen without geographic barriers: through specialization on different host plants or food sources, through mate choice for divergent traits, or abruptly through chromosome duplication, which in plants can create a new species in a single generation. The process is usually gradual, and the boundary between varieties and species is often blurred.
