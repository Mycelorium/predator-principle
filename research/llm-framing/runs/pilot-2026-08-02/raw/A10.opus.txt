Good outcomes in a multi-agent system come less from the intelligence of individual agents than from the structure around them. Clear, narrow objectives matter: agents optimizing vague goals drift or pursue proxies that diverge from what anyone wanted. Interfaces need to be explicit, so one agent's output is legible to the next and errors surface rather than propagate silently. Diversity helps, but only with mechanisms that aggregate disagreement — review, voting, adversarial checking — instead of letting confident mistakes cascade. Bounded authority is essential: each agent should hold only the permissions its task requires, with irreversible actions gated behind human confirmation. Feedback loops must be short enough that failures are caught while they're cheap, and observable enough that someone can reconstruct why a decision was made. Finally, the system needs a stopping condition and a coordinator willing to enforce it, since the characteristic failure of autonomous agents is not doing too little but grinding on past the point of usefulness.
