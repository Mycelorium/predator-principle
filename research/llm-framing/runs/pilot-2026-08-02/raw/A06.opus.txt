Ants and termites owe their dominance to eusociality: reproduction is concentrated in a few queens while sterile workers forage, build, defend, and rear brood. This division of labor lets a colony act as a single large organism, buffering individual death and allowing perennial persistence, rapid growth, and simultaneous exploitation of many food patches. Chemical communication through pheromone trails and caste-specific signals coordinates thousands of individuals without central control, producing collective foraging, nest construction, and coordinated defense that solitary insects cannot match. Both groups engineer their environments, building climate-controlled nests and moving vast quantities of soil, and both have evolved farming symbioses, ants tending fungi and aphids, termites cultivating fungus gardens or hosting gut microbes and protists that digest cellulose. That last trait opened the enormous, poorly contested resource of dead plant matter. Combined with small body size, dietary flexibility, and ancient origins, these traits let them occupy nearly every terrestrial habitat and rival vertebrates in total biomass.
