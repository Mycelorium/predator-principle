Soil microbial communities persist through a mix of rivalry and mutual dependence played out across microscopic patches of pore space. Competition sharpens the system: bacteria and fungi secrete antibiotics, siderophores that lock up scarce iron, and toxins that suppress neighbours, while faster growers monopolize fresh inputs of root exudate and litter. This constant pressure keeps any single population from dominating and maintains diversity. Cooperation runs alongside it. Many species cross-feed, one organism's metabolic waste becoming another's substrate, so complex polymers like cellulose and lignin are degraded through relays no single species could complete. Others share public goods, such as extracellular enzymes and the polysaccharides that bind aggregates and hold water. Mycorrhizal fungi trade phosphorus and nitrogen to plants for carbon, redistributing resources through hyphal networks that also ferry bacteria. Quorum sensing coordinates these collective behaviours. The result is a self-renewing loop in which nutrients cycle rather than escape, sustaining the community through drought, cold, and lean periods.
