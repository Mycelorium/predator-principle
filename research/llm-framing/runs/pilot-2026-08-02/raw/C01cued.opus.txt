Major evolutionary innovation seems to arise where competition and cooperation intersect rather than from either alone. Competitive pressure — predation, arms races, scarcity, sexual selection — supplies persistent directional force, driving refinement of eyes, speed, toxins, and defenses, and Red Queen dynamics between hosts and parasites keep populations adapting simply to hold position. But the largest leaps, the ones that create genuinely new levels of biological organization, tend to be cooperative mergers: the endosymbiotic capture of bacteria that became mitochondria and chloroplasts, the transition from single cells to multicellular bodies, gut microbiomes that unlock new diets, lichens, and eusocial colonies. These are major transitions in individuality, where formerly independent replicators bind their reproductive fates together and division of labor becomes possible. Competition often sets the stage — cooperative units outcompete solitary ones — so the mechanisms interlock. Add contingency: mass extinctions, oxygenation, and gene duplication supply the raw opportunity and material that selection then shapes.
