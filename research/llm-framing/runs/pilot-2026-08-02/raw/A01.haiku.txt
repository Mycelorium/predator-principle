Complex cells with mitochondria arose through endosymbiosis, a process where a larger anaerobic cell engulfed a smaller aerobic bacterium. Rather than being digested, this bacterium was retained within the host cell's cytoplasm, establishing a symbiotic relationship. The engulfed bacterium gradually lost its independence, with its genes transferred to the host nucleus and its membrane system simplified into the mitochondrial double membrane. This arrangement proved mutually beneficial: the host cell gained access to efficient ATP production through aerobic respiration, which was vastly more energy-productive than anaerobic metabolism. The bacterium gained protection and steady nutrient supply from the host. Over evolutionary time, this temporary association became permanent and obligate. Evidence supporting this theory includes mitochondria possessing their own DNA, ribosomes resembling bacterial ribosomes, and the double membrane structure reflecting the original engulfment event. Similar endosymbiotic events explain the origin of chloroplasts in photosynthetic eukaryotes.
